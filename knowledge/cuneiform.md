# Cuneiform

## Origins
Cuneiform began in southern Mesopotamia (Uruk) around 3400–3200 BCE as a bookkeeping system pressed into clay with a reed stylus. Early signs were pictographic; over centuries they became abstract wedge-shaped (Latin *cuneus*, "wedge") signs that mix logograms and syllables.

## Languages and spread
It wrote Sumerian, then Akkadian (Babylonian and Assyrian dialects), and was adapted for Elamite, Hittite, Hurrian and Urartian. Old Persian cuneiform (Achaemenid period, c. 6th century BCE) is a separate, simplified semi-alphabetic system. The latest securely dated tablets are astronomical texts from the 1st century CE.

## Decipherment
Georg Grotefend made early progress on Old Persian in 1802. Henry Rawlinson copied the trilingual Behistun inscription (Old Persian, Elamite, Babylonian), which, with work by Edward Hincks and Jules Oppert, unlocked Akkadian in the mid-19th century.

## Reading notes
Tablets are small, read in lines left to right, and often damaged. Genre ranges from receipts and letters to omens, law codes and literature (e.g. the Epic of Gilgamesh). Sign identification depends heavily on period and region, so expert transliteration is essential.

Sources: Britannica "Cuneiform"; British Museum, "Cuneiform"; Walker, *Cuneiform* (1987).
